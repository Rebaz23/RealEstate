1 · An oil economy trying to diversify

Iraq remains one of the most oil-dependent economies in the world. In 2025 oil made up roughly 53% of real GDP, 88% of government revenues, and 91% of merchandise exports (World Bank estimates). Growth therefore tracks OPEC+ quotas more than domestic activity: 2025 saw a small oil-led contraction, while the faster unwinding of production cuts is projected to lift growth to around 5.1% a year in 2026–27. The non-oil economy — where real estate lives — grew only ~1.5% in 2025, held back by electricity and water shortages and a liquidity squeeze when oil receipts fell.

Two structural features matter for any consumer platform. First, the public sector dominates employment, so household income is tightly coupled to the state budget — and hence to oil. Second, reconstruction spending in Baghdad, Basra, Nineveh and Anbar, plus growing Gulf partnerships in energy and transport corridors, is where most non-oil momentum currently comes from.

2 · Money: a managed dinar and a cash-first system

The Central Bank holds the dinar at an official ~1,300 IQD/USD (the 2026 budget rate); the market rate sat near 1,310 in July 2026, though parallel-market spreads have reached 15–20% in stress periods. Inflation moderated to ~4.3% in 2025.

The bigger story is how payments work. An estimated 87% of Iraq's money supply (~95 trillion IQD, ≈ $72.5B) sits outside the banking system, and thousands of exchange offices outnumber the country's ~900 bank branches. But the direction of travel is clear: the CBI says financial inclusion passed 40% in 2025, roughly double earlier levels; government payments went electronic-only in June 2025; and a CBI restructuring plan (with Oliver Wyman) forces banks to meet new governance standards, merge, or lose their licenses — scheduled to complete in 2026.

3 · People: young, urban, and concentrated in a few cities

Iraq's 48 million people have a median age of just 21.1 — about 36% are under 15 — and 73% live in cities. Metro Baghdad alone holds ~8 million people (one in six Iraqis); Basra exceeds 1.6M, Erbil tops 1M, and the three Kurdistan Region governorates together hold ~6.25M. Every year, a large cohort of young Iraqis enters household-formation age in a market that is already millions of units short — the demographic engine behind persistent housing demand.

4 · Real estate: severe shortage, opaque prices, supply finally moving

Official and advisory estimates put the national housing shortage at 2.5–3 million units, with Baghdad missing roughly 31% of the units it needs — the worst gap in the country. Prices reflect it: affluent Baghdad districts (Jadriya, Mansour, Yarmouk) trade at $3,000–7,000 per m² — rivaling European capitals — while lower-income areas like Sadr City run $680–1,360 per m². Statista sizes the total market around $1.17 trillion (2025), ~79% of it residential, growing ~3.7% a year.

Supply is finally responding. The government's 2025–2030 plan aims to cut the shortage in half via new residential cities; the flagship, Bismayah New City (planned 100,080 units southeast of Baghdad), has built ~30,000 and handed over ~20,000. The Housing Ministry credits the new-cities program with a ~10% drop in property prices — the first meaningful cooling after years of increases.

5 · What this means for Rasedi

Demand is structural, not cyclical

A 2.5–3M-unit shortage plus the world's youngest big-country median age means search volume and household formation keep growing regardless of the oil cycle. The user funnel is durable even when GDP isn't.

Price opacity is the product opportunity

Per-m² prices vary 5–10× across Baghdad districts with no reliable public benchmark. AI-driven valuation, the trust score, and cross-referencing office claims against real-user signals attack exactly the market failure buyers feel most.

Design for cash, ride the digital wave

With ~87% of money outside banks, closing a transaction stays offline for now — but inclusion doubling to 40%+ and mandatory e-payments mean lead-gen today can become payments/escrow tomorrow. Sequence the roadmap accordingly.

New supply is a B2B wedge

Government cities (Bismayah and successors under the 2025–2030 plan) release thousands of near-identical units through offices and developers — ideal inventory for the B2B portal's bulk listing and AI-qualified-lead pipeline.

Watch the oil-linked liquidity channel

Household purchasing power moves with public salaries, which move with oil. A soft-oil year (like 2025) shows up as slower closings and price cooling — worth building into demand forecasts and office-side expectations.
